An international team has measured how much helium formed in the first few minutes after the Big Bang. Their uncertainty is just 0.5%, which the team says is about three times more precise than earlier estimates. This primordial helium abundance is one of the three main lines of evidence for the Big Bang. A tighter value gives physicists a sharper test of the Standard Model, the theory that describes the known elementary particles and forces.

## What happened

The results appear in five papers in *The Astrophysical Journal*, according to a University of Minnesota release published by ScienceDaily on October 20, 2026. The work is called the LBT Yp Project. "Yp" is the usual symbol for the primordial helium mass fraction, the share of ordinary matter by mass that was helium before any stars formed. The project drew on 130 hours of observations with the Large Binocular Telescope (LBT) in Arizona.

Earlier studies usually observed many galaxies and then extrapolated back to estimate the helium level before stars changed it. Stars fuse hydrogen into helium and release it, so the helium seen in most galaxies today is higher than the original amount. This team took a different approach. It picked 15 small, distant galaxies that are unusually "pristine," meaning they have gone through little chemical evolution and still hold gas close to its original makeup.

The observations used the Multi-Object Double Spectrographs (MODS), built at Ohio State University. These instruments recorded more than 10 helium emission lines and 15 hydrogen lines at once. Analysing so many lines together let the researchers find and correct small systematic errors that earlier studies could largely miss. Errors of that size matter once the target uncertainty drops below 1%. Ohio State astronomer Richard Pogge said the instruments took 12 years to go from concept to first light.

The collaboration includes researchers from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities, Ohio State, Gonzaga, Northwestern, the University of Texas at Austin, Indiana University, UC Santa Cruz, the University of Illinois, the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, and TRIUMF, Canada's particle accelerator centre. Evan Skillman of the University of Minnesota leads the first paper.

## Why it matters

In Big Bang nucleosynthesis, protons and neutrons fused into light elements such as helium and deuterium during the Universe's first minutes. How much helium formed depends on conditions at that time, including how fast the Universe was expanding. That expansion rate in turn depends on how many kinds of light particles were present. Measuring helium precisely therefore puts limits on the early Universe's particle content.

The team used its helium value to estimate the number of neutrino families in the early Universe. Neutrinos are nearly massless subatomic particles. The Standard Model has three types, or "flavours." If the helium data pointed to a number other than three, it could hint at new physics, such as extra light particles. Skillman called the measurement "one of the biggest findings in my entire 40-year career."

The other two main pillars of Big Bang evidence are the expansion of the Universe and the cosmic microwave background, the leftover radiation from the early Universe. Both have been studied in great detail. The release notes that primordial helium has had comparatively less attention, so a large gain in precision fills a notable gap.

## What's uncertain

The release does not give the measured helium fraction itself or the resulting number of neutrino families. It also doesn't say whether either result agrees with Standard Model predictions or with values derived from the cosmic microwave background. Those numbers presumably appear in the papers, but the announcement doesn't report them, so readers can't yet judge whether the result confirms standard physics or creates any tension with it.

The "three times more precise" claim comes from the researchers. The release doesn't say which earlier estimates it is compared against. As with any precision measurement, independent analyses will need to check how the team handled systematic errors. Skillman noted that the project met the half-percent target set in its original proposal.
