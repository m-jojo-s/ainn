Physicists at the University of Innsbruck and France's CNRS describe a "fractional Fermi sea," an exotic quantum state that ultracold cesium atoms can reach when the forces between them are repeatedly switched from strong repulsion to strong attraction. The atoms end up with far more energy than in their lowest-energy state but stay highly ordered. According to the researchers, that ordering does not fit the standard theory used for one-dimensional quantum systems.

## What happened

The findings appear in *Physical Review Letters*. The work comes from Hanns-Christoph Nägerl's group in the University of Innsbruck's Department of Experimental Physics and theoretical physicist Alvise Bastianello of the CNRS and Université Paris-Dauphine. Yi Zeng is the lead author.

**Note on the headline:** the published paper is theoretical, according to the University of Innsbruck's release. It lays out the framework and calculations for fractional Fermi seas. The release says a companion paper on creating the state in an experiment is still under peer review. Until that paper is published, the claim that the state has been "observed" rests on work that has not yet passed peer review.

The system is a gas of cesium atoms cooled to near absolute zero and confined to one dimension, so the atoms can only move along a single line. Researchers can tune how strongly ultracold atoms interact. The team modelled what happens when those interactions are cycled smoothly between strong repulsion and strong attraction, starting from the ground state (the system's lowest-energy configuration).

One might expect such driving to simply heat the gas into disorder. The calculations show something different: the atoms rearrange into a new collective "many-body" state that is highly excited but still structured. "This state is highly excited, but it is not random," Nägerl said.

## Why it's called a "fractional Fermi sea"

Fermions, the class of particles that includes electrons, cannot share a quantum state. At low temperatures they fill the available energy levels one by one from the bottom up, and that filled stack is called a Fermi sea. In the new state, the particles appear to follow a modified version of that filling rule, which is where the name "fractional" comes from. The release does not say exactly how the rule changes.

Some background helps here, although the release does not mention it. Cesium-133, the isotope Nägerl's group usually works with, is a boson rather than a fermion. In one dimension, strongly repelling bosons are known to behave in many ways like fermions. The same group's earlier experiments used a similar switch from repulsion to attraction to produce a related excited state, the "super-Tonks-Girardeau" gas.

## Why it matters

The researchers point to two signs of hidden order:

- **Friedel oscillations:** strong ripple-like patterns in the gas that come from the way the particles affect one another.
- **Unusual correlations:** the link between particles weakens with distance in an unexpected pattern, even when the repulsion between them is fairly weak.

Neither feature matches Tomonaga-Luttinger liquid theory, the standard framework for interacting particles in one dimension. On that basis, the authors describe the fractional Fermi sea as a new "critical phase" of matter that the standard framework cannot fully explain.

The work also concerns quantum simulation, in which tightly controlled quantum systems such as ultracold atoms are used to study physics that is hard to access in other ways. The authors argue that the method can be used to design new states, not just to recreate known models.

## What's uncertain

- **Experimental confirmation:** this is the main open question. The supporting experiment has not yet been peer reviewed, and the release gives no measurements, atom numbers or other experimental details.
- **The new quasiparticles:** quasiparticles are collective excitations that behave like single particles. Their nature in this state is unsettled. "We are not yet sure how we should name these new quasiparticles. Perhaps 'super-Fermions'?" Nägerl said. That name is a suggestion, not an established term.
- **Missing details:** the release does not give the exact occupation rule, how stable or long-lived the state is, or the publication date and title of the paper.
- **Applications:** the release claims no practical uses. For now this is basic research into how quantum matter behaves far from equilibrium, meaning when a system has not settled into a stable balance.
