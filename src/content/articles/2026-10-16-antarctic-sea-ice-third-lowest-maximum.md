Antarctic sea ice reached its 2026 winter peak on September 14. It covered about 17.59 million square kilometers (6.79 million square miles), the third-lowest maximum since satellite records began in late 1978. The figures come from NASA and the National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC). They add to a decade-long run of low ice around Antarctica, after the region's sea ice had grown slightly for decades while Arctic ice shrank fast.

## What happened

Sea ice is frozen seawater floating on the ocean. It is separate from Antarctica's ice sheet and ice shelves, which form from snow on land. Around Antarctica, sea ice spreads across the Southern Ocean during the southern winter, usually peaks in September and shrinks to its smallest extent in February.

NASA Earth Observatory's report says the 2026 peak "likely" happened on September 14. That word is common in early sea ice reports, because the date and size of the yearly peak can be revised slightly after more data are processed and checked.

The total is a measure of "sea ice extent." That is the ocean area where ice covers at least 15 percent of the surface. Scientists use this threshold to decide which areas count as ice-covered.

Antarctica's geography shapes how its ice behaves. In the Arctic, the ocean is mostly ringed by land. Antarctica is a continent surrounded by open ocean, so its sea ice has room to spread far outward in winter. That also carries the ice into warmer latitudes, where much of it melts each summer.

## From record highs to record lows

From 1979 to 2014, Antarctic sea ice extent grew by about 1 percent per decade. That was the opposite of the steep losses in the Arctic. Antarctic ice changes a lot from year to year, though, so researchers have never been sure the small increase was a real long-term trend.

The growth was clearest from 2012 to 2014, when winter sea ice set new record highs three years in a row. The upward trend ended in 2015, and from 2016 the region lost a lot of ice. Some years have recovered a little, but extent has mostly stayed below the historical average since then.

The lowest point so far was 2023. That year set records for both the smallest summer minimum and the smallest winter maximum in the satellite era. The 2026 maximum is the latest data point in that pattern.

## How the ice is measured

The long-term record relies on satellite instruments called passive microwave sensors. They do not take pictures in visible light. Instead, they detect the microwave energy that Earth's surface gives off naturally. Sea ice gives off a stronger microwave signal than open water, which lets scientists tell the two apart. Microwaves also pass through clouds, so the ice can be tracked even under the thick cloud that often covers the Southern Ocean.

The record has been kept going by several missions:
- **NASA's Nimbus-7**, from late 1978
- **Defense Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP)** satellites, from 1987
- **NASA's Aqua** satellite, from 2002 to 2011
- **The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's GCOM-W** satellite, whose data researchers began adding in 2025

## What's uncertain

The main open question is whether the losses since 2016 mark a lasting change in Antarctic sea ice or fall within the region's large natural swings. NASA says scientists will keep tracking the ice each year to find out. The report gives no explanation for why the 2026 maximum was so low. It does not discuss possible causes such as ocean warming, wind patterns or atmospheric circulation. It also does not say which year holds the second-lowest maximum, beyond naming 2023 as the lowest.

Background context helps explain why this matters, though the report does not go into it. Sea ice already floats, so its melting does not directly raise sea levels. But it reflects sunlight, affects how heat moves between the ocean and the air, and supports Antarctic ecosystems. Scientists also study whether less sea ice leaves the coastal ice shelves more exposed to waves and warmer water, which could matter for the land ice behind them. How strongly the recent losses affect these processes is still being researched.
