Governments have voted to end the leap second right away and replace it with a "leap hour." Under the new rule, atomic time and Earth's rotation can drift up to an hour apart before anyone adjusts the clocks. Member states of the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), the treaty body that oversees the international system of units, passed the resolution on 15 October 2026 at their 28th meeting, held outside Paris. Timekeepers have argued for decades that the occasional extra second causes more trouble than it is worth, and this vote settles that argument for a very long time.

## What happened

Since 1972, clocks have occasionally added one second to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). UTC is the global time standard, based on atomic clocks. The extra second keeps it in step with time measured by Earth's rotation. Earth's spin is irregular, so it slowly drifts away from atomic time. Nature says these adjustments have happened roughly once every 18 months on average. By widely reported counts, 27 leap seconds have been added, the last at the end of 2016.

The CGPM resolution scraps that system immediately. Instead, UTC will shift by one full hour once the gap between Earth time and atomic time reaches 3,600 seconds. Nature reports that this probably won't happen for thousands of years, so in practice the problem passes to future generations.

The resolution needed only a simple majority, and it was not unanimous. Nature did not say how many countries voted for or against, or which ones objected. Canada supported it, according to Georgette Macdonald, director general of the Metrology Research Centre at the National Research Council of Canada. Metrology is the science of measurement. Noël Dimarcq, a metrologist at the Côte d'Azur Observatory in Nice, France, formally introduced the resolution and called it "a historical moment, a decisive step for UTC."

## Why it matters

Leap seconds have long annoyed the people who run precise timing systems. Adding a second at short notice can break computer software and leave clocks briefly out of step with each other on the day it happens. Those problems matter more as telecoms networks, financial trading, satellite navigation and data centres depend on tightly synchronised time. Supporters say that without the jumps, UTC will run continuously for centuries or longer, which makes it more dependable for these uses.

Nature's headline describes the change as a way to avoid a catastrophic "negative second." The free part of the article doesn't explain this. Background reporting does: Earth's rotation has recently been speeding up slightly. If that continued, timekeepers might one day have to take a second away from UTC for the first time. Most software has never been tested for a missing second, and many engineers think it could do more damage than an added one.

The vote also closes a long-running debate. In 2022, at its 27th meeting, the CGPM agreed to stop adding leap seconds by 2035 but left the replacement undecided. The new resolution moves the end date forward and names the leap hour as the successor.

## What's uncertain

A large part of Nature's article, including a section about computing casualties, is behind a paywall. This summary is based only on the freely available text, so some details of the decision and the reactions to it are not covered here. Points that remain unclear include:

- **The vote count:** the number of countries voting each way, and why the opponents objected.
- **How it will work in practice:** it's not clear how standards bodies, operators of satellite navigation systems such as GPS, and software developers will switch over, or how they will handle a UTC that is no longer kept within a second of Earth's rotation.
- **When a leap hour would be needed:** the timing depends on how Earth's rotation changes over the long term, and scientists cannot forecast that precisely. "Thousands of years" is an estimate.
- **How it would be done:** the free text doesn't say how a one-hour adjustment would be carried out, or whether anyone would ever actually make one.

For now, the gap between atomic time and solar time is expected to grow slowly, by seconds per century. Astronomers and other specialists who need precise Earth-rotation time already use separate time scales for it.
