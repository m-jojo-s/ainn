A new global analysis estimates that venomous snakebites kill between 274,000 and 513,000 people every year. That is several times the figure most often cited for global snakebite deaths. The researchers say the real toll has stayed hidden because more than half of the countries where venomous snakes are a threat don't count bites at all. They also find that sub-Saharan Africa carries a much larger share of the burden than earlier work suggested.

## What happened

The study appears in *PLOS Medicine*. Its authors are researchers in snakebite and tropical disease epidemiology. One of them, health data scientist Dileepa Ediriweera of the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine, described the work in an article for The Conversation, which ScienceDaily republished on October 30, 2026. The source doesn't give the paper's exact publication date or the full author list.

The team reviewed studies published up to March 31, 2025, covering 79 countries. The work updates the same group's widely cited 2008 estimate of the global burden. The study is about envenoming, meaning cases where a bite actually delivers venom. Many snakebites are "dry" and inject none.

To fill gaps where no records exist, the researchers used model-based geostatistics. This method predicts values for places without data by combining the data points that do exist with other information, such as:

- climate
- how many snake species live in an area
- socioeconomic indicators

The team also compared hospital and regional statistics with community surveys. Many victims never reach a clinic, so the surveys helped them estimate how many cases go unreported. The result is a range, from a minimum to a plausible maximum, rather than a single figure.

The main estimates:

- **Envenomings:** at least 2.1 million a year, and possibly as many as 7 million.
- **Deaths:** 274,000 to 513,000 a year, after adjusting for under-reporting.
- **Disability:** more than 800,000 survivors a year left with lasting physical harm, including amputations.
- **Geography:** sub-Saharan Africa accounts for 45% of global envenomings and deaths. Its rates are nearly three times those in south Asia. Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Ethiopia are named as high-burden countries.

## Why it matters

The World Health Organization (WHO) has commonly put annual snakebite deaths at roughly 81,000 to 138,000. If the new range is correct, the true toll is two to six times higher. That would make snakebite one of the deadlier neglected tropical diseases. WHO added it to that category in 2017. It has also set a goal of halving deaths and disability from snakebite by 2030. The authors argue that country-level maps of where the burden falls are the first step toward that goal.

The authors say sub-Saharan Africa's high toll has structural causes. The region has many medically important venomous species, including:

- saw-scaled vipers
- puff adders
- African cobras
- mambas

Many farm workers there labour in open fields without protective boots and walk unlit paths at night. After a bite, people may face long journeys to a clinic, weak health systems, and antivenom that is expensive or unavailable. Some victims go to traditional healers instead and never appear in official records. The authors note that similar conditions exist in parts of Asia.

The researchers say the maps could guide decisions about where to stockpile antivenom, where to train primary healthcare workers, and where faster emergency transport is most needed. Ediriweera writes that better data "cannot solve the problem," but can help direct resources to the communities that need them most.

## What's uncertain

The width of the ranges reflects how little direct data there is. The upper and lower envenoming estimates differ more than threefold, and the death estimates almost twofold. Much of the global picture comes from modelling rather than counted cases, especially in countries with no surveillance. That means the results depend on the model's assumptions and on how well community surveys represent the wider population.

The summary doesn't give confidence intervals, country-by-country figures beyond the three named countries, or how the new totals compare directly with the team's 2008 numbers. Some of the gap between the new figures and older ones may come from different methods, not only from a larger burden. Other researchers and health agencies will need to check whether the higher estimates hold up.
