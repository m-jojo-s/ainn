Researchers in the UK report that about 3.5% of people with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) make antibodies that block interleukin-10 (IL-10), a protein the immune system uses to keep inflammation in check. The study, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, also links these antibodies to a genetic risk factor tied to severe IBD 30 years ago. It points to IBD being several separate diseases that start in different ways, at least one of which may be found with a blood test and treated at its cause.

## What happened

A team from the University of Oxford, Newcastle University and Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust tested more than 4,900 people with IBD. IBD is the umbrella term for Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, two long-term inflammatory conditions of the gut. About 500,000 people in the UK have IBD, and millions more worldwide.

Normally IL-10 acts as a signalling molecule (a cytokine) that tells immune cells to calm down. The researchers found raised levels of "neutralising" autoantibodies against IL-10 in roughly 3.5% of patients. Autoantibodies are antibodies that attack the body's own molecules. When they block IL-10, the body loses one of its built-in brakes on inflammation, and gut inflammation can keep going unchecked. The antibodies turned up in people with Crohn's disease and with ulcerative colitis, but not in the healthy people tested. Going by how common IBD is in the UK, the researchers estimate that 15,000 to 20,000 patients there may carry them.

The team also found a strong link between the antibodies and a gene variant called HLA-DRB1\*01:03. HLA genes help the immune system tell the body's own tissue from foreign invaders. Oxford researchers tied this variant to severe IBD about three decades ago, but no one knew how it caused harm. The new data suggest that people who carry it are much more likely to make antibodies against IL-10, which would explain the link.

## How the finding was built

The work grew out of research on rare inherited disorders. More than ten years ago, scientists found that some young children with severe IBD had genetic defects in IL-10 or its receptor. Later, Newcastle immunologist Sophie Hambleton and colleagues found two young girls with colitis whose antibodies against IL-10 had the same effect as those inherited defects. The new study suggests this antibody-driven route to disease is not limited to a handful of rare cases.

Senior author Holm Uhlig, director of Oxford's Centre for Human Genetics, said the study supplies "the missing link" between the known gene variant and the recently found autoimmunity to IL-10.

## Why it matters

Many people with IBD are first diagnosed as teenagers or young adults. They often go through several treatments, including immune-suppressing drugs, costly biologic therapies, hospital stays and sometimes surgery, before anything works for long. If doctors can identify a subgroup whose disease has a known cause, they could test for it and pick treatment based on what is driving the inflammation rather than on symptoms.

The researchers say their results could form the basis of a blood test for the IL-10 antibodies. They suggest that future treatments could remove or neutralise the antibodies themselves, or target the immune cells that produce them. Rainer Doffinger, a clinical immunologist at Cambridge, argued that catching these patients early could cut use of biologic drugs, hospital admissions and surgery, saving the NHS many millions of pounds a year. Oxford gastroenterologist Simon Travis called it the most exciting discovery of his career in IBD.

## What's uncertain

The press release does not give some key details. It doesn't say what share of carriers of the gene variant make the antibodies, whether the antibodies come before the disease or follow it, or how the healthy comparison group was chosen. The blood test and the targeted treatments are proposals, not products that have been tested in patients. It is not yet known whether removing the antibodies or the cells that make them would improve symptoms. The estimate of 15,000 to 20,000 UK patients is extrapolated from the study group rather than counted directly. The cost-saving figures are the researchers' projections. The study was funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) Biomedical Research Centres in Oxford, Cambridge and Newcastle.
