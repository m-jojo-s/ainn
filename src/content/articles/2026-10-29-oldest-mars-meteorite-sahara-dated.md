A rose-colored rock found in the Sahara in 2022 is a piece of Mars that formed more than 4.1 billion years ago, according to a NASA-funded study. That makes it one of the oldest Martian meteorites ever identified. Researchers say the stone, named Teghaza 001, offers a rare sample of Mars' ancient crust from a type of terrain no earlier meteorite has come from. It also preserves evidence of how water and rock interacted early in the planet's history.

## What happened

The meteorite weighs about 800 grams (nearly 2 pounds). It was found on a salt plain in Mali and named after it. For some time after the find, its age and origin were unknown. Researchers at the University of New Mexico first identified it as Martian by its oxygen isotopes, the ratios of oxygen variants that act as a chemical fingerprint for different planetary bodies. At first it looked like a shergottite, the most common class of Martian meteorite.

Yang Liu of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) led the closer analysis. Her team found unusual textures and minerals, including zircon crystals, which geologists use to date rocks precisely. To date the sample, the group used in-situ rubidium-strontium dating, a method developed by Caltech coauthor François Tissot's team. Bulk methods dissolve a whole sample. This technique instead measures single mineral grains on a fragment smaller than a fingernail, leaving most of the rare meteorite intact for future research.

The results showed that Martian water altered the rock around 4.1 billion years ago, so the rock itself must have formed even earlier. The team's first description of the meteorite was published in *Science* on Thursday, October 29, 2026. A separate paper on the meteorite's water content appeared in *Science Advances* in June.

"We've only scratched the surface," Liu said, adding that one reviewer had called the rock a Rosetta Stone for Mars.

## Why it matters

About 435 meteorites from Mars are known. All of them were blasted off the planet by impacts and eventually landed on Earth. Very few date from Mars' earliest era. NASA says Teghaza 001 is one of only three of the most ancient Martian meteorites, and the other two show less of the planet's history:

- **ALH84001** is about the same age but is made almost entirely of one mineral.
- **"Black Beauty"** formed about 2.1 billion years ago, according to NASA, and consists of small volcanic rock fragments.

Teghaza 001 contains a mix of igneous minerals, meaning minerals that crystallized from molten rock. Together they record how Mars' crust formed as magma cooled and was later changed by underground water. The rock's mineralogy matches what NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has seen in the planet's heavily cratered southern highlands. Lee Saper, the paper's second author, called it the first sample of crust from a region of Mars never sampled before. Saper did the work as a JPL postdoctoral scholar and is now at NASA's Ames Research Center. Saper also said it may preserve a trace of the Martian atmosphere from the period when Mars was losing its water.

The researchers say the meteorite's oxygen isotopes, texture and chemistry point to a more active water cycle on early Mars than some earlier views suggested. Tissot said his lab is one of very few able to perform this kind of dating. He hopes the method can be used on more Martian meteorites and on samples returned from other planets, moons and asteroids.

## What's uncertain

- **Exact origin:** It is unknown, and NASA says it will likely never be known, where on Mars the rock came from. The team identified 13 sites with matching mineralogy and chemistry.
- **Exact formation age:** The roughly 4.1-billion-year figure dates the water alteration. NASA's release says only that the rock formed earlier and gives no specific crystallization age.
- **Early findings:** The *Science* paper is described as an initial characterization. Conclusions about early Mars' water cycle and atmosphere are early interpretations, and the researchers expect more studies by other scientists.
- **Missing details:** NASA's summary does not give the full author list, the error ranges on the dates, or the specific ages measured from the zircons.
