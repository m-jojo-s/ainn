UN climate projections now show global temperatures passing 2 °C above pre-industrial levels around the middle of the century unless countries cut emissions much faster. The report comes from the UN Environment Programme (UNEP) and was released on 21 October 2026. It found that global greenhouse-gas emissions were about level from 2024 to 2025, which may be the first fall since the COVID-19 pandemic. Even so, current policies point to warming far beyond the limits set in the Paris Agreement.

## What happened

UNEP publishes this report every year to track how the world is doing against its climate goals. Nature reported that this edition puts expected warming at 2.6–2.8 °C by the end of the century under current policies. That is well above the 2015 Paris Agreement's ceiling of "well below" 2 °C. The report also gives a roughly 10% chance that warming tops 3.4 °C on the current path, an outcome researchers call catastrophic. Even if every country kept all of its existing emissions pledges, the report projects 2.1 °C of warming.

The report treats the stricter Paris goal of 1.5 °C as out of reach. It expects 2026 to be the second year in which temperatures rose above that level. However, it says an "overshoot" may still be possible. This means temperatures go past 1.5 °C for a while and are then brought back down through very fast decarbonization and a large expansion of carbon dioxide removal (methods that take CO₂ out of the air and store it).

At the launch press briefing, outgoing UN secretary-general António Guterres pointed to the heat waves, wildfires and floods that hit many regions in 2026. He urged governments to move away from fossil fuels, build more clean electricity and reverse deforestation. "The emissions gap is now a leadership gap," he said. The emissions gap is the difference between where emissions are heading and where they need to be to meet the temperature goals.

## The bright spots

Early estimates suggest emissions in 2025 matched or fell slightly below 2024 levels. Most of the change came from land use, especially lower wildfire emissions after record fires in 2024. Fossil fuel use did not drive it. A flat or falling year could mean emissions are levelling off or have peaked, but researchers warn that one year is not enough to show a lasting trend.

Lauri Myllyvirta, a researcher at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air in Helsinki, told Nature that emissions would need to fall by several per cent before anyone could be confident they had peaked. Until then, he said, a very hot summer or another short-term swing could push emissions above the earlier high, even if the underlying trend is downward.

The report also points to fast growth in renewable energy. Since 2015, clean-energy capacity has grown about three times faster than expected, and solar about seven times faster, helped by falling costs. The report projects emissions in 2030 will be 5–8% below 2025 levels. Myllyvirta argued that much bigger cuts are possible over the next five years because clean energy is speeding up, fossil fuels are getting more expensive, and China and India could beat their pledged emissions cuts.

## Why it matters and what's uncertain

The report is published shortly before COP31, the UN climate summit due in November in Antalya, Turkey. There, governments will review their efforts to cut emissions and adapt to climate change. The new figures make clear that current national plans, even if fully carried out, fall short of the Paris targets.

Several important questions remain open:
- **The 2025 figures are early estimates.** They could change.
- **The plateau rests mainly on lower wildfire emissions**, which can vary a lot from year to year. It may not reflect lasting changes in energy or industry.
- **Coming back down from an overshoot depends on carbon dioxide removal at a very large scale.** This has not yet been shown to work at the scale needed.
- **Part of the coverage was not available.** Nature's article sits partly behind a paywall, including a section on the gap between pledges and what is needed. Specific numbers from that part of the report, such as the size of the emissions gap in gigatonnes, are not included here.

Together, the findings show early signs that emissions are slowing and that clean energy is growing faster than expected. But the world remains on course for warming well above the targets governments agreed in 2015.
