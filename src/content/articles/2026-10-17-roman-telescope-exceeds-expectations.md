NASA engineers say the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope's main camera is producing sharper images than its specification called for. After focusing and alignment work in orbit, the Wide Field Instrument measured an optical error of 63 nanometers RMS, compared with a pre-launch requirement of about 90 nanometers. That's an improvement of roughly 30%. Roman's planned surveys of dark matter and dark energy depend on this camera, so the result is an early sign that the observatory should be able to meet its main science goals.

## What happened

Roman launched on August 30. According to the NASA release republished by ScienceDaily, the Wide Field Instrument was switched on in September. Engineers then tested it part by part:

- **Detectors:** they checked the instrument's 18 detectors, each about the size of a sticky note, and the electronics that run them.
- **Element wheel:** this rotating mechanism holds filters and other optical parts that select wavelengths of light or split it into its colors.
- **Calibration system:** onboard LEDs, controlled from the ground, produce known colors and brightness levels. Measuring how each detector responds lets scientists correct for differences in sensitivity.
- **Focus and temperature:** they moved the alignment mechanism from the deliberately unfocused position used for launch to the position expected to give the sharpest images, then checked the thermal control system.

A separate group worked on the alignment of the telescope's mirrors and other optics. Matthew Bolcar, Roman's optical systems lead at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center, said the optics were already performing very well before any adjustment. He credited ground testing and modeling by Goddard and the contractors L3Harris and BAE Space and Mission Systems.

The correction that was needed was tiny. Senior optical engineer Alden Jurling said the team moved the secondary mirror about one-sixteenth of the width of a human hair, which changed how high it sits above the primary mirror. He compared it to raising the Empire State Building by slightly less than the thickness of a penny.

The team first fixed the Coronagraph Instrument. A coronagraph blocks the glare of a bright star so that faint nearby objects, such as planets, can be seen. Its images had been slightly out of focus, but less than engineers expected, and NASA says it now meets performance expectations. The team then returned to the Wide Field Instrument for further adjustments. The end result was the 63-nanometer measurement.

## What the numbers mean

"RMS wavefront error" measures how far incoming light strays from a perfectly focused wavefront, averaged over the optical system. Lower is better. The 90-nanometer requirement is about 1,000 times thinner than a human hair. Getting the error down to 63 nanometers means stars and galaxies should look more compact and well defined.

For Roman, that matters in a specific way. Its plan to study dark energy and dark matter relies partly on measuring tiny distortions in the shapes of very large numbers of galaxies, so sharper images should give more precise measurements. Jurling summed it up: "All of the images from Roman's cosmic surveys will be even sharper than we expected."

## What's uncertain

- **Requirement or prediction?** The headline says performance beat *predictions*, but the only figure given before launch is the 90-nanometer *requirement*. The roughly 30% gain is the difference between 63 and 90. The release doesn't say whether engineers had separately predicted a value closer to 90, or whether "prediction" is being used to mean the requirement.
- **Commissioning isn't finished.** In the coming weeks the team plans to test how well the coronagraph blocks unwanted light. Engineers are also still choosing camera settings and configurations for its deformable mirrors, which change shape to make very fine corrections to incoming light.
- **No timeline given.** The release doesn't give a date for the start of routine science operations. It also doesn't say whether the alignment is expected to stay stable over the mission, though Goddard engineer Maxime Rizzo said the aim is to set the instrument up and then leave its settings alone.
- **Early checks only.** These are engineering measurements from commissioning, the checkout period before normal operations. NASA has not yet released any science images or survey data.

Bolcar also pointed out that ground tests can't fully reproduce conditions in space, such as weightlessness and differences in temperature and moisture. He said both instruments still matched or beat the performance engineers had predicted.
