Older adults with a particular genetic makeup who took daily low-dose aspirin developed dementia at about one-third the rate of similar people on a placebo, according to a new analysis from Monash University. The same group also had about twice the rate of major bleeding while on aspirin. The researchers say the result is not a reason to start taking aspirin. What it does suggest is that genetics might one day show who could benefit from a cheap, widely available drug that showed no overall dementia benefit in earlier trials.

## What happened

The study appears in *Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association*. It used genetic data from more than 13,500 participants in ASPREE (Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly). ASPREE was a large placebo-controlled trial of daily low-dose aspirin in healthy older people in Australia and the United States. Its main results, published in 2018, found no overall benefit and a higher risk of bleeding. The new paper is titled "Platelet count polygenic scores may modify the effect of aspirin in primary prevention of dementia." Peter Fransquet, a research fellow at Monash's School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine, is the lead author. The co-authors include John McNeil and Paul Lacaze.

The team gave each participant a polygenic score, which adds up the small effects of many genetic variants into one number. These scores estimated each person's inherited tendency toward a higher or lower platelet count. Platelets are blood cell fragments that help blood clot, and aspirin works partly by stopping them from clumping together.

The researchers then compared dementia rates between the aspirin and placebo groups at different score levels. The clearest signal was in people with the top 20% of platelet-count scores:

- **Dementia:** 1.0% of aspirin users vs 3.3% of placebo users, about a 70% lower relative risk
- **Major bleeding:** 4.4% of aspirin users vs 2.1% of placebo users

By AINN's calculation from these figures, the absolute changes were about the same size. Dementia fell by about 2.3 percentage points and major bleeding rose by about 2.3 points. The 70% headline figure is a relative reduction, so it sounds much larger than the absolute difference.

## Why it matters

The researchers say the signal did not come from the well-known genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease and other dementias. It points instead toward platelet biology. Earlier research has linked dementia to platelet activation and clumping, but much less is known about any link with overall platelet count. "It's pointing us towards platelet biology and gives us a new avenue to investigate," Fransquet said.

The finding could also help explain why trials that pooled everyone found no dementia benefit from aspirin. A real effect in one genetic subgroup can disappear when results are averaged over a whole population. If the result is confirmed, genetic testing might be used to pick out people for targeted prevention instead of giving everyone the same advice. The release notes the scale of the need: Dementia Australia estimates about 446,500 Australians are living with dementia in 2026, a number expected to pass one million by 2065.

## What's uncertain

- **It's a secondary analysis.** ASPREE was not designed to test aspirin in people grouped by platelet-related genes. Results from subgroups found after a trial ends are prone to false positives, especially when several groups are tested.
- **Missing details.** The press release does not say how many people were in the top-20% group, how many dementia cases there were, or how statistically precise the estimates are. It also does not say how long participants were followed or what ancestry they had. Polygenic scores often work less well outside the populations they were built on.
- **Trade-offs.** The higher bleeding risk in the same group means any benefit would have to be weighed carefully. Major bleeds in older adults can be serious.
- **Next steps.** The authors say the result has to be repeated in other groups of people. After that, it would need a trial that enrols people with this genetic profile from the start.

Fransquet warned people not to start taking aspirin to prevent dementia based on this study without talking to their doctor, mainly because of the bleeding risk. If the finding holds up, he said, the fact that aspirin is cheap and easy to get could make personalized dementia prevention realistic.
