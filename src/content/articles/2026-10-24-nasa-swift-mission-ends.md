NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has ended its science mission after almost 22 years in orbit, the agency announced on Oct. 23, 2026. The spacecraft has dropped below 171 miles (275 kilometers), and at that altitude air drag keeps its telescopes from holding steady on their targets. Swift was built to catch gamma-ray bursts within about a minute of detecting them. Its shutdown leaves astronomy without a dedicated fast-response observatory, and NASA has not yet named a replacement.

## What happened

Swift made its final observation on Oct. 20, NASA said. The agency expects it to fall back into the atmosphere within the next few weeks. NASA did not give a specific re-entry date or say whether any debris is expected to survive.

Spacecraft in low Earth orbit slowly lose height because the thin upper atmosphere drags on them. Drag gets stronger as a satellite sinks, so the decline speeds up near the end. For Swift, the problem was pointing. Its instruments need to stay locked on a source, and increasing drag made that impossible.

Swift launched on Nov. 20, 2004, and began science operations the next month. It was designed for a two-year primary mission. Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director of the Astrophysics Division at NASA Headquarters, said Swift stayed productive "for more than 10 times as long." The observatory was renamed in 2018 for Neil Gehrels, its first principal investigator, after his death.

## What Swift accomplished

Gamma-ray bursts are the most powerful explosions known. They flare and fade in seconds to minutes, which is too fast for large telescopes to point at them in time. Swift was designed to solve that problem. It would detect a burst, work out where it was, send the position to other observatories, and turn its own X-ray and optical/ultraviolet telescopes to the spot before the fading glow left behind (the "afterglow") disappeared. Principal investigator S. Bradley Cenko of NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center said the whole sequence usually took about a minute.

That speed paid off most with short gamma-ray bursts, which peak in under two seconds. In May 2005, Swift caught GRB 050509B, a burst lasting just 0.03 seconds. It turned toward the burst fast enough to detect 11 X-ray photons, making it the first short burst with a detected afterglow. NASA says those observations supported long-standing theories that short bursts likely come from mergers of two neutron stars. Neutron stars are collapsed stellar cores with about the Sun's mass packed into an object the size of a city.

Over its lifetime, NASA says, Swift:

- observed more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts
- studied more than 1,400 supernovae
- watched stars being torn apart by black holes
- monitored active galaxies
- completed an all-sky X-ray survey
- observed asteroids and comets

Its data contributed to about 7,000 published scientific papers.

Swift's scheduling system was another reason it was so widely used. Astronomers could request "target-of-opportunity" observations, meaning unplanned looks at sudden events, and these could be commanded from the ground within minutes. Monitoring campaigns could last anywhere from minutes to months, and the system could handle up to 75 separate targets per day. Operations director John Nousek of Penn State credited the hardware's fast response and the close teamwork of the flight and science operations teams.

## What's left behind

Swift's public archive holds about 12 terabytes of data:

- nearly 22 years of brightness measurements of cosmic outbursts
- spectra across multiple wavelengths
- more than 1.5 million images from its Ultraviolet/Optical Telescope

NASA expects the archive to support research for decades.

## What's uncertain

The biggest open question is what replaces Swift's quick-reaction role. Domagal-Goldman said NASA is looking at three options:

- making more use of, and upgrading, its existing space telescopes
- "fast-to-orbit" commercial capabilities
- new, efficient missions

NASA says it will use current missions to help cover the gap for now. It has not announced specific replacement missions, budgets or timelines.

The announcement also doesn't explain why the orbit couldn't be saved. In 2025, NASA publicly announced a contract with a commercial company for a robotic mission to boost Swift into a higher orbit. The new release doesn't mention that effort or say what became of it, so it's unclear whether a reboost was attempted, called off or overtaken by the orbit's decline.

Finally, NASA has not said how much the end of Swift's alert role will affect "multi-messenger" astronomy. That is the practice of studying the same event through different signals, such as gravitational waves and light. Short-burst afterglows like the ones Swift pioneered are central to it, and rapid X-ray and ultraviolet follow-up has relied heavily on Swift. How well other observatories can fill that gap is still unknown.
