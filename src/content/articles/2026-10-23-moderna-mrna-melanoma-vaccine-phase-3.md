A personalised mRNA cancer vaccine from Moderna and Merck, given together with the immunotherapy drug pembrolizumab, cut the combined risk of melanoma returning or the patient dying by 42% in a phase III trial. That is a larger effect than pembrolizumab alone produced. If regulators approve it, the vaccine, called intismeran, could become the first approved mRNA vaccine against cancer. It would also show that mRNA technology can do more than the infectious-disease vaccines it became known for during the COVID-19 pandemic.

## What happened

The trial included 1,137 people with moderate to advanced melanoma, the most dangerous form of skin cancer, at 157 centres. All of them had already had surgery to remove their tumours. Patients were randomly assigned to one of two groups. One group got intismeran plus pembrolizumab, a checkpoint inhibitor already widely used for melanoma. Checkpoint inhibitors release a brake on the immune system so it can attack tumours. The other group got pembrolizumab plus an inactive placebo.

The 42% figure is for a combined endpoint: either the cancer came back or the patient died. It does not measure recurrence by itself. Trial leader Georgina Long, an oncologist at Melanoma Institute Australia in Sydney, said patients have been followed for about 25 months so far. They will be monitored for several more years to see whether the vaccine helps people live longer overall. She said the treatment caused no major harmful side effects.

Intismeran is not meant to stop melanoma from developing. It is meant to keep the cancer from returning after surgery. Each dose is made for one patient. Researchers sequence DNA from the patient's tumour to find mutations that produce neoantigens, which are abnormal proteins found on cancer cells but not on healthy ones. The vaccine then trains the immune system to recognise those targets. Long said the combination is aimed at people whose cancer could come back after surgery and who do not respond to immunotherapy alone.

The full data are due at the European Society for Medical Oncology meeting in Madrid on 24 October 2026. The companies released the main results early, after data leaked online on 22 October. They had first announced positive top-line findings in August.

## Why it matters

Independent researchers called the size of the benefit unusually large. Kristen Radford, an immunotherapist at Mater Research in Brisbane, said oncologists treat a 10% reduction in risk as substantial. Seth Cheetham, a geneticist at the University of Queensland, said a reduction above 40% "would send many researchers over the Moon".

Melanoma treatment has improved a great deal in recent years. Long noted that about 15 years ago, people with advanced melanoma usually survived only a few months. Today, close to 55% are effectively cured with targeted drugs or immunotherapy. A vaccine that adds to these gains could help patients the current drugs do not help enough.

## What's uncertain

Several important questions are still open:

- **How long the benefit lasts.** Jessamy Tiffen, a cancer geneticist at the Centenary Institute in Sydney, said only time will show how well the treatment works beyond two years. She warned that cancer cells can adapt to get around treatments.
- **Overall survival.** It is not yet known whether the vaccine helps people live longer. Answering that will take more years of follow-up.
- **Who benefits.** Tiffen noted that some patients did not respond to the vaccine. Long said her team will run a statistical analysis to find out which patients are most likely to benefit.
- **Detailed data.** The released results do not give full breakdowns, such as absolute recurrence rates or results for subgroups of patients. These are expected at the Madrid presentation.
- **Manufacturing.** The source suggests that making a separate vaccine for each patient is difficult. Its full discussion of this was behind a paywall, so cost, production time and scale cannot be confirmed here.

Investors responded unevenly. Moderna's share price had risen after the August announcement but fell about 25% after the leak. Evan Seigerman, a health-care analyst at BMO Capital Markets, told Forbes the drop probably reflected inflated expectations, not a judgement that the treatment doesn't work.
